Understand the threat

Phishing exploits trust.

Phishing is a deceptive message that tries to make you reveal sensitive information, send money, open a harmful attachment, or visit a fake website. It often impersonates someone you trust. Social engineering is the broader practice of manipulating people into taking an unsafe action.

Where it appears

  • Email phishing: fake account alerts or invoices.
  • Smishing: phishing through SMS or messaging apps.
  • Vishing: deceptive voice calls.
  • QR phishing: a QR code hides a risky destination.

How it targets you

  • Spear phishing: tailored to your role or interests.
  • Whaling: targets senior decision makers.
  • Business email compromise: impersonates or misuses business accounts to redirect payments or data.

Good grammar, your real name, a familiar sender, and a professional logo do not prove a message is safe. A genuine account can also be compromised.

Social engineering

Recognize the pressure tactic.

Urgency & fear

“Your account closes in 10 minutes.” Pressure reduces the time you spend checking.

Authority & impersonation

“I’m your manager. Buy gift cards now.” A trusted role is used to override normal checks.

Reward & curiosity

“You won a scholarship. Pay a processing fee.” An attractive offer encourages impulsive action.

Familiarity & secrecy

“We met at the workshop. Keep this request private.” Personal detail builds trust; secrecy isolates you from advice.

Safer habit: pause and verify through a separate channel you already trust.

Practice: inspect a message

Can you spot the warning signs?

Fictional training sample. All example domains use the reserved .example suffix. No sample link is active.

From: Campus IT <support@campus-help.example>
Subject: FINAL WARNING: mailbox disabled in 15 minutes

Hello student,

Your college mailbox has failed verification. Confirm your password and OTP immediately to keep access.

Displayed button: Verify college account
Actual destination: https://college.example.verify-access.example/login

Do not contact the help desk while verification is in progress.

Select each clue to reveal why it matters.

Fake websites

Read the address, not the branding.

  • Check the actual hostname. In college.example.verify-access.example, the controlling domain is verify-access.example, not college.example.
  • Look for subtle changes. Extra words, swapped characters, and misleading subdomains can imitate a trusted address.
  • HTTPS is not a trust guarantee. It encrypts the connection to that site; a phishing site can use HTTPS too.
  • Inspect before opening. Hover on desktop or use your device’s link preview without navigating. If the destination is unclear, use the official app or a trusted bookmark.
  • Question unexpected requests. A page asking for an OTP, recovery code, payment, or software installation needs independent verification.
  • Do not trust a QR code automatically. Check the destination preview; a physical sticker or trusted-looking email can still be fraudulent.
Real-world examples

Common scams, familiar situations.

These are paraphrased patterns documented by public safety sources, not copies of actual messages.

Case 1 · The missed delivery text

A text claims your package needs a small redelivery payment. Its link opens a courier look-alike page asking for card details. The FTC documented this pattern in April 2025.

Warning signs: unsolicited payment request, unexpected link, and a delivery problem you have not verified.

Safe response: check tracking through the retailer’s account or the courier’s official app, opened independently.

Read the FTC example
Case 2 · Your subscription is “on hold”

The FTC’s phishing guide describes a branded billing-problem email that encourages a payment-details update. Familiar branding and a generic greeting are part of the disguise.

Safe response: open your subscription account directly and check billing there. Do not use the message’s link to resolve the alert.

Read the FTC guide
Case 3 · The urgent executive request

NIST warns that phishing can impersonate a business leader. In this illustrative scenario, a “manager” asks an employee to send confidential files urgently and skip normal approval.

Safe response: call the manager using a known number and follow the organization’s approval process, even if the email address looks familiar.

Build safer habits

Pause. Verify. Report.

Before you act

  • Check the full sender address and actual link destination.
  • Verify unusual requests through a known phone number or official app.
  • Never share passwords, OTPs or recovery codes with someone contacting you.
  • Do not enable macros or install remote-access software on an unexpected request.
  • Use unique passwords, a password manager, updates and MFA. Prefer phishing-resistant options such as security keys or passkeys where supported.
  • Use your organization’s phishing-report button or established IT/security channel.

If you already interacted

  1. Stop interacting. Do not enter more information or approve new prompts.
  2. Report promptly. Tell IT/security what happened, when, and what you shared.
  3. If credentials were entered: use the official service on a trusted device to change the password, revoke sessions and review MFA settings. Change reused passwords too.
  4. If a file was run: contact IT immediately; disconnect the affected device from the network and follow their incident instructions.
  5. If payment data or money was involved: contact your bank or payment provider immediately through its official channel.

Clicking a link does not automatically mean an account is compromised. Your response depends on whether you entered information, downloaded or ran a file, or granted access. Report honestly and quickly.

Your takeaway

A message can look convincing and still be dangerous. Check the request, verify independently, and report anything suspicious. You do not need to investigate a suspicious website yourself.
